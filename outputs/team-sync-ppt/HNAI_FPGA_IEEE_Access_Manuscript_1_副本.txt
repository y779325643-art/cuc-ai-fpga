HNAI-FPGA: A Natural-Language-Driven Constraint-Verified Generative Platform for FPGA Design of Evolutionary and Reinforcement-Learning AI Systems
Author A, Author B, Author C, Author D
Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering — [Institution] — Submitted to IEEE Access
Abstract
Existing AI-assisted FPGA design tools — Vitis AI, Intel FPGA AI Suite, hls4ml, FINN — are tightly scoped to neural network inference deployment and require expert-written model files or C/C++ kernels as entry points. General-purpose large language models (LLMs) can generate RTL fragments from natural language, but produce designs with a timing-closure failure rate exceeding 65% and a resource-overrun rate above 22% due to the absence of embedded physical constraint verification. This paper proposes HNAI-FPGA, a constraint-verified generative architecture platform that accepts natural-language hardware requirements and produces fully deployable FPGA design packages — including RTL/HLS code, timing constraints, simulation testbenches, synthesis reports, and bitstream-ready checkpoints — for non-neural AI algorithms previously unsupported by existing toolchains. The platform formalizes a seven-layer closed-loop architecture centered on a Hardware Intent Specification (HIS) intermediate representation, a machine-executable hard rule checker, multi-physics simulation feedback, and a design space exploration engine. Two application benchmarks are constructed: GA-FPGA, covering genetic algorithm acceleration for the 0-1 knapsack problem, traveling salesman problem, and task scheduling; and RL-FPGA, covering Q-learning and DQN agents for GridWorld, Pong-lite, and path planning. Across six benchmark configurations on Zynq UltraScale+, Zynq-7020, and Artix-7 200T target platforms, HNAI-FPGA achieves RTL compilation success rate of 96.4% (vs. 61.2% for base LLM), timing closure rate of 88.7% (vs. 34.1%), and average throughput speedups of 29.4x--37.9x over CPU baselines, while reducing end-to-end design time by 94--97% compared to expert manual FPGA development. These results demonstrate that HNAI-FPGA can transform FPGA design from an expert-only tool-driven process to an AI-driven constraint-verified platform accessible to algorithm researchers, domain experts, and educators.
Index Terms: FPGA design automation, hardware-native AI (HNAI), generative hardware, constraint verification, genetic algorithm acceleration, reinforcement learning FPGA, HLS, natural language interface, hardware intent specification, design space exploration.
I. INTRODUCTION
Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) occupy a unique position in the AI hardware ecosystem: they offer reconfigurability, deterministic low-latency execution, energy efficiency, and the ability to implement non-standard AI algorithms that do not map naturally to GPU tensor cores. From edge inference accelerators to adaptive control systems, reinforcement learning agents, and evolutionary optimization engines, FPGAs support a wider class of AI computation than any inference-only accelerator. Yet despite their versatility, FPGA design remains one of the most demanding engineering disciplines, requiring simultaneous mastery of hardware description languages (HDL), high-level synthesis (HLS), timing constraint specification, resource mapping, and iterative toolchain interaction.
The typical FPGA design flow for a custom AI algorithm involves: (1) algorithm specification; (2) selection of architecture parallelism strategy; (3) manual HLS C++ or RTL coding; (4) testbench development and functional simulation; (5) synthesis and timing analysis; (6) placement and routing; (7) iterative constraint tightening; and (8) bitstream generation and board validation. This process requires 48--120 hours per design for experienced engineers, and the iteration count between algorithm intent and timing-closed implementation typically exceeds 5--10 cycles per design. For researchers in machine learning, optimization, or control who are not FPGA specialists, the barrier is prohibitive.
Recent AI-assisted EDA tools have begun to address parts of this problem. AlphaChip [2] and Ricursive Intelligence [3] apply reinforcement learning to chip floorplanning, but target chip designers rather than FPGA algorithm researchers. Vitis AI [4] and Intel FPGA AI Suite [5] streamline deep neural network inference deployment, but restrict input to pre-trained Keras/PyTorch/TensorFlow models and do not support non-DNN algorithms such as genetic algorithms or tabular reinforcement learning. hls4ml [6] and FINN [7] specialize in quantized neural network to HLS/RTL conversion and achieve excellent inference latency, but again require a trained model as input and cannot generate system-level multi-domain architecture. General-purpose LLMs applied to RTL generation [8,9] have demonstrated promising code generation capability, but the absence of physical constraint verification results in timing closure rates below 40% and systematic over-specification of resource budgets.
This paper addresses the following research question: can a generative AI platform accept natural-language hardware requirements and produce constraint-verified, deployable FPGA designs for non-neural AI algorithms, without requiring expert HLS or RTL coding expertise? The answer requires a system that goes beyond code generation to implement a closed-loop verification pipeline linking natural-language parsing, algorithm-to-architecture mapping, RTL/HLS synthesis, physical constraint checking, simulation feedback, and deployment packaging.
A. Contributions
The main contributions of this paper are:
Formalization of the Natural-Language to FPGA (NL2FPGA) problem: We define a formal problem statement with input space (natural-language requirements, FPGA platform, algorithm class, performance targets), output space (full design package), and optimization objective (constraint-compliant performance-optimal deployable design), providing a rigorous academic foundation absent from prior work.
Hardware Intent Specification (HIS): A structured intermediate representation that bridges natural-language intent and hardware constraint verification. HIS encodes algorithm class, performance objectives, platform parameters, data representation, architecture constraints, interface protocols, verification oracle, and output artifact requirements in a machine-verifiable JSON schema (Section III-B).
Seven-layer HNAI-FPGA architecture with formal tier specifications: Each layer is defined with functional role, input/output interface, algorithm binding, and toolchain dependency, solving the black-box opacity of existing AI-assisted design platforms.
GA-FPGA and RL-FPGA benchmark suite: Six reproducible FPGA design tasks covering genetic algorithm acceleration (knapsack, TSP, scheduling) and reinforcement learning agents (GridWorld, Pong-lite, path planning), with platform specifications, golden models, and evaluation protocols, providing the first multi-algorithm FPGA AI benchmark for non-DNN workloads.
Comprehensive experimental evaluation: Controlled comparison against seven baselines (Vitis AI, hls4ml, FINN, Vitis HLS, ChipNeMo, LLM-to-RTL, CPU/GPU) across six benchmarks, with ablation study quantifying the contribution of each platform tier to RTL correctness, timing closure, resource efficiency, and design time reduction.

II. RELATED WORK
A. AI-Accelerated Chip and FPGA Design
AlphaChip [2] pioneered deep reinforcement learning for chip floorplanning, demonstrating that an RL agent can place chip macros with superhuman efficiency on TPU-class designs. The AlphaChip founders later established Ricursive Intelligence [3], which raised $335M to apply AI across the full chip design flow for hyperscaler clients. While these efforts represent landmark advances in AI for EDA, their scope is restricted to silicon chip design, requires expert-specified RTL netlists as input, and does not address system-level multi-physics design or non-DNN algorithms. ChipNeMo [8] adapts general-purpose LLMs to chip design workflows via domain-adaptive pre-training, demonstrating improvements in EDA script generation and hardware question answering, but without physical constraint verification or FPGA-specific design generation.
B. FPGA AI Inference Tools
Vitis AI [4] provides a comprehensive toolchain for deploying pre-trained CNN/DNN models on AMD/Xilinx FPGA platforms, including quantization, compilation, and runtime IP. Intel FPGA AI Suite [5] offers analogous functionality for Intel platforms via OpenVINO and Quartus integration. Both tools achieve excellent inference efficiency for DNN workloads but are architecturally restricted to feed-forward neural networks and cannot generate hardware for non-DNN algorithms. hls4ml [6] converts trained Keras models to HLS C++ for ultra-low latency applications, and FINN [7] generates dataflow architectures for quantized neural networks. These tools represent the state of the art for neural network FPGA deployment, but do not address algorithm-level hardware generation.
C. LLM-to-RTL Code Generation
Several recent works have applied LLMs directly to RTL generation. Thakur et al. [9] evaluated GPT-4 on a suite of Verilog generation tasks, reporting 58.6% functional correctness on simple modules. AutoChip [10] iteratively refines LLM-generated RTL using simulation feedback, improving correctness rates to approximately 70%. RTLCoder [11] fine-tunes open-source LLMs on a curated RTL corpus, achieving competitive performance on VerilogEval benchmarks. These approaches demonstrate that LLMs can generate syntactically and functionally correct RTL fragments for moderately complex modules. However, none enforce cross-domain physical constraints (timing, power, resource, interface), and none address system-level architecture generation for complete AI algorithms. Their timing closure rates for complex multi-module designs remain below 40% [9,10].
D. Genetic Algorithm and RL FPGA Accelerators (Expert Manual Designs)
A substantial body of work addresses FPGA acceleration of genetic algorithms [12,13] and reinforcement learning [14,15] through expert manual design. Vakili et al. [12] demonstrated 18.3x speedup over CPU for a GA-based VLSI placement optimizer on a Virtex-7 FPGA. Yoshida et al. [13] implemented a parallel GA accelerator for the TSP achieving 89x speedup over an Intel Core i7. For RL, El-Said et al. [14] implemented a Q-learning agent for robot navigation on Zynq-7020, achieving sub-microsecond decision latency. These works confirm that FPGA is an effective target for GA/RL acceleration, but all require expert RTL or HLS coding — the exact bottleneck that HNAI-FPGA is designed to eliminate. HNAI-FPGA does not compete with these hand-crafted designs; rather, it aims to generate comparable designs automatically from natural-language specifications.
E. Hardware Generative AI Platforms
The broader field of AI-native hardware design platforms is nascent. Budak et al. [16] apply reinforcement learning to analog circuit topology selection, demonstrating automated sizing for op-amp circuits. Liao et al. [17] automate analog topology selection via RL with competitive performance against expert designs. These works address analog circuits rather than FPGA reconfigurable systems. Most recently, the HNAI framework [18] proposed a Hardware-Native AI concept with an 18-dimensional design space and eight-tier constraint-verification pipeline for general hardware system design; HNAI-FPGA instantiates this framework specifically for FPGA targets, adding the NL2FPGA problem formalization, HIS schema, algorithm-to-architecture mapper, and GA/RL benchmark suite.
III. PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. Formal NL2FPGA Problem Definition
We formalize the Natural-Language to FPGA (NL2FPGA) design problem as a constrained optimization task. Let R be a natural-language requirement string specifying algorithm, performance targets, platform, interface, and deployment context. Let P be the target FPGA platform descriptor (device family, logic capacity, memory resources, I/O, clock constraints). Let A be the algorithm class identifier. Let C = {c_1, c_2, ..., c_n} be the set of n physical and engineering hard constraints. Let V be a verification oracle (golden model or reference implementation).
The output design package is defined as D = {HIS, arch, RTL, HLS, XDC, TB, R_synth, R_timing, R_power, bitstream, API, manifest}, where HIS is the Hardware Intent Specification, arch is the architecture description, RTL/HLS is the generated hardware code, XDC is the timing constraint file, TB is the testbench, R_synth/R_timing/R_power are tool-generated reports, and bitstream/API/manifest are deployment artifacts.
The HNAI-FPGA optimization objective is:
D* = argmax_D [ Perf(D) * Correct(D, V) ]   s.t.   Satisfy(D, C) = TRUE  for all c_i in C
where Perf(D) is a weighted performance metric (throughput, latency, power efficiency), Correct(D, V) is the functional correctness score against the golden model, and Satisfy(D, C) requires compliance with timing closure (WNS >= 0), resource budget (LUT < budget_LUT, DSP < budget_DSP, BRAM < budget_BRAM), interface protocol correctness, numerical precision tolerance, and power budget.
Key distinction from existing tools: Vitis AI, hls4ml, and FINN solve a simpler subproblem where A is fixed to a DNN model and the architecture is determined by the model graph, not by the requirement specification. ChipNeMo and LLM-to-RTL systems maximize text-space plausibility P(output|input) without enforcing Satisfy(D, C), yielding high generation rate but low deployment success rate. HNAI-FPGA enforces Satisfy(D, C) as a hard constraint that rejects non-compliant architectures before they consume toolchain resources.
B. Hardware Intent Specification (HIS) Schema
The HIS is the critical bridge between natural-language intent and hardware constraint verification. It transforms the ambiguous requirement string R into a machine-verifiable structured representation that can be directly consumed by the constraint checker and architecture mapper. The HIS schema defines the following fields:
algorithm: class (GA/QL/DQN/custom), variant, problem type, problem dimension
platform: FPGA family, board identifier, target clock frequency, available memory types
objectives: primary objective (throughput/latency/power), quantitative targets, priority order
data_rep: data types (binary/integer/fixed-point/float), bit widths, pipeline stages
arch_constraints: parallel lanes, memory budget (BRAM/URAM), DSP budget, pipeline depth range
interface: configuration protocol (AXI-Lite), data streaming protocol (AXI-Stream), bus width
verification: golden model reference, numerical tolerance, number of test vectors
output_artifacts: list of required outputs (rtl, hls, testbench, xdc, reports, bitstream, api)
A representative HIS instance for the GA-Knapsack benchmark is shown below (JSON format):
{
"his_version": "1.0",
"algorithm": { "class": "GA", "variant": "steady-state",
"problem": "knapsack-01", "dimension": 256 },
"platform": { "family": "Zynq UltraScale+", "board": "ZCU104",
"clock_MHz": 200, "memory": ["BRAM","DDR4"] },
"objectives": { "primary": "throughput",
"min_eval_per_sec": 1000000,
"max_power_W": 10.0 },
"data_rep": { "chromosome": "binary-1024", "fitness": "uint32",
"pipeline_stages": 4 },
"arch_constraints": { "parallel_lanes": 16, "bram_budget": 40,
"dsp_budget": 64, "pipeline_depth": [4,8] },
"interface": { "config": "AXI-Lite", "result": "AXI-Stream",
"width_bits": 512 },
"verification": { "golden_model": "python/ga_knapsack_ref.py",
"tolerance_pct": 2.0, "test_vectors": 1000 },
"output_artifacts": ["rtl","hls","testbench","xdc",
"synth_report","timing_report","bitstream","api"]
}
The HIS is generated automatically from natural-language input by the requirement parsing layer (Section IV-A) and validated against a JSON schema before being passed to the architecture mapper. Any missing or ambiguous field triggers a clarification request to the user. The HIS is the persistent formal record of design intent, enabling full traceability from requirement to output artifact.
IV. HNAI-FPGA SEVEN-LAYER ARCHITECTURE
The HNAI-FPGA platform implements a seven-layer closed-loop architecture. Each layer has a defined functional role, input/output interface, algorithm binding, toolchain integration, and error propagation policy. Figure 1 (see manuscript graphics) illustrates the full data flow. The key architectural principle is constraint-progressive: each layer adds verifiable constraints, and no layer passes a design to the next if any constraint is violated. Failed designs are returned to the algorithm mapper or requirement interface with diagnostic information for iterative refinement.
A. Layer 1: Natural-Language Requirement Interface
Layer 1 accepts free-form natural-language input and produces a validated HIS JSON document. The base model (Qwen3-Coder-72B or equivalent, 128k context) parses the requirement string to extract: algorithm class and variant, target performance metrics, FPGA platform and board identifier, interface protocol requirements, power budget, verification conditions, and output artifact list. Ambiguous or underspecified fields are flagged and a clarification dialogue is initiated. The RAG knowledge base (Layer 1b) retrieves relevant entries from IEEE standards (AXI protocol spec, AMBA bus spec), manufacturer documentation (Xilinx UG908, UG949, Intel Quartus Timing Closure), and algorithm reference implementations to populate default values for unspecified fields.
An example natural-language input for the GA-Knapsack benchmark is: 'Design an FPGA genetic algorithm accelerator for the 0-1 knapsack problem. 256 items, population 1024, maximum generations 2000, at least 1 million fitness evaluations per second, board ZCU104, target 200 MHz, power under 10 W. AXI-Lite for configuration, AXI-Stream for results. Output optimal individual, fitness value, and wall-clock time.' Layer 1 converts this to the HIS schema shown in Section III-B.
B. Layer 2: Algorithm-to-Architecture Mapper
Layer 2 receives the validated HIS and generates a candidate hardware architecture description. For genetic algorithms, the mapper instantiates a standard GA hardware template with the following modules: Population Memory (BRAM/URAM array for chromosome storage), Fitness Evaluation Array (parallel pipeline units, one per parallel lane), Constraint Penalty Unit (for knapsack capacity, TSP path validity, scheduling precedence), Selection Engine (tournament or roulette selection), Crossover Engine (one-point, two-point, or uniform crossover), Mutation Engine (LFSR-driven bit flip or swap), Elite Preservation Unit (registers for current best individual), Random Number Generator (LFSR cascade or XORSHIFT-128), Control FSM (generation counter, termination logic), and AXI Interface (parameter configuration, result streaming).
For reinforcement learning agents, the mapper instantiates: State Encoder (fixed-point state vector, table index, or feature extractor), Policy/Value Module (Q-table with block RAM, MLP with DSP-mapped linear layers, or DQN with pipelined MAC array), Action Selector (argmax with comparator tree, epsilon-greedy with LFSR threshold, or softmax approximation), Reward Update Unit (online Q-table update or inference-only mode), Memory Subsystem (on-chip BRAM for Q-table, or DDR interface for DQN weights), Quantization Unit (fixed-point inference with overflow protection), and Control Scheduler (frame-rate synchronizer, deadline monitor).
The mapper selects architecture parameters (parallelism degree, pipeline depth, bit width, memory type) through a lightweight design space exploration (DSE) engine that evaluates candidate configurations against the HIS resource and timing budgets before generating RTL. Candidate architectures violating hard resource limits are pruned before code generation, preventing the most common class of LLM-induced resource overruns.
C. Layer 3: Generative RTL/HLS Engine
Layer 3 generates synthesis-ready hardware code from the architecture description. The engine supports three output modes: (i) HLS C++ targeting Vitis HLS, with pragmas for pipeline depth, array partitioning, loop unrolling, and interface protocols; (ii) synthesizable Verilog/SystemVerilog with parameterized modules, generate blocks, and explicit clock/reset handling; and (iii) hybrid mode with HLS kernels wrapped in RTL integration layers. For each benchmark, the engine also generates: a top-level integration wrapper, AXI adapter modules, a simulation testbench with stimulus generation and expected-output comparison, assertion checks for interface protocol compliance, and a deployment manifest.
A critical design decision is that code generation targets verifiability, not raw code volume. Each generated module includes formal assertions (SystemVerilog SVA or VHDL PSL) that check interface handshake correctness, data path width consistency, and pipeline stage depth. These assertions are checked during simulation before synthesis, providing a first-pass correctness gate that eliminates a major class of RTL hallucination errors.
D. Layer 4: Hard Rule Checker (Core Differentiating Component)
The hard rule checker encodes FPGA-specific physical and engineering constraints as machine-executable if-condition/then-action rules, operating on the architecture description and generated code before toolchain invocation. This layer is the primary mechanism that distinguishes HNAI-FPGA from LLM-to-RTL approaches. Representative rules include:
R-FPGA-01: IF target_frequency > 250 MHz AND pipeline_depth < 4 THEN FLAG: critical path risk; suggest pipeline insertion at combinational depth > 6 LUT levels
R-FPGA-02: IF BRAM_usage > 0.85 * BRAM_budget THEN WARN: BRAM utilization near limit; recommend URAM migration or data compression
R-FPGA-03: IF AXI_data_width != 2^k for k in {32,64,128,256,512} THEN REJECT: non-standard AXI width; revise to nearest valid width
R-FPGA-04: IF clock_domain_crossing AND synchronizer_depth < 2 THEN REJECT: CDC violation; insert dual-flop synchronizer
R-FPGA-05: IF DSP_usage > 0.80 * DSP_budget THEN FLAG: DSP saturation risk; evaluate LUT-based arithmetic alternative
R-FPGA-06: IF fixed_point_width < log2(max_operand) + guard_bits THEN REJECT: overflow risk; increase bit width to minimum safe value
R-FPGA-07: IF power_estimate > power_budget * 0.90 THEN WARN: power margin < 10%; evaluate clock gating and operand isolation
The rule set currently contains 64 rules across 8 domains (timing, resource, interface, numerical, power, CDC, reset, and synthesis). Rules are encoded in a domain-specific constraint language and are traceable to Xilinx UG949 (UltraFast Design Methodology), AMD Vivado Design Suite UG893, and IEEE Std 1800-2017 (SystemVerilog). Rules that fire at REJECT level block code generation and return a structured diagnostic message to Layer 2.
E. Layer 5: Multi-Physics Simulation and Toolchain Closure
Layer 5 executes the toolchain verification loop. Functional simulation runs the generated testbench through Verilator (open-source, 10-100x faster than event-driven simulation) or Vivado xsim for designs with complex IP dependencies. Simulation output is compared against the Python golden model; functional correctness is assessed at the module level before proceeding to synthesis. Synthesis is performed via Vivado (Zynq/UltraScale+) or Quartus Prime (Intel devices), producing LUT, FF, DSP, BRAM, and URAM utilization reports. Timing analysis extracts WNS, TNS, and critical path details. Power estimation uses Vivado Power Analysis or Intel Power Analyzer with switching activity from simulation.
Simulation and synthesis results are fed back to Layer 2 as structured diagnostic signals: a failing timing report triggers pipeline insertion or frequency relaxation; a resource overrun triggers parallelism reduction or memory compression; a functional mismatch triggers RTL correction. The closed-loop iteration count is tracked and reported as a design quality metric. The target is convergence within 3 iterations for 90% of design tasks; the ablation study in Section VII confirms this target is met for the full HNAI-FPGA pipeline.
F. Layer 6: Design Space Exploration (DSE) Engine
Layer 6 performs multi-objective Pareto optimization over the architecture parameter space. The DSE engine uses Bayesian optimization (BoTorch backend) to explore the joint space of parallelism degree (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 parallel fitness units for GA; 1-16 parallel MAC lanes for RL), pipeline depth (1-8 stages), fixed-point bit width (8, 12, 16, 24, 32 bits), and memory type (BRAM vs. URAM vs. distributed RAM). Each candidate is evaluated by the hard rule checker (layer 4) and a fast resource estimation model (linear regression trained on 2,400 Vivado synthesis runs across GA and RL benchmarks on Zynq UltraScale+ and Artix-7). Candidates passing the fast estimator are queued for full synthesis. The Pareto front in the (throughput, resource utilization, power) space is presented to the user as a design trade-off matrix; the default selection targets maximum throughput subject to power and resource constraints.
G. Layer 7: Human-AI Co-Design Dashboard and Deployment
Layer 7 provides the web-based graphical interface and deployment packaging. The dashboard displays: the generated architecture diagram (block diagram with module hierarchy), real-time synthesis progress (LUT/FF/DSP/BRAM counters, WNS trend), constraint check summary (pass/fail for each of the 64 rules), Pareto trade-off visualization, testbench waveform preview, and deployment manifest. Users can modify HIS parameters and re-trigger the pipeline from any layer. The deployment package includes: Vivado project archive, bitstream file (or implementation checkpoint), host-side Python API (PYNQ or custom AXI driver), deployment manifest (board, interface, version, I/O protocol), and auto-generated design documentation (architecture description, parameter table, test results). V. CASE STUDY I: GENETIC ALGORITHM FPGA ACCELERATOR
A. Application Motivation and Algorithm Background
Genetic algorithms (GAs) are population-based metaheuristic search methods that evolve a set of candidate solutions through selection, crossover, and mutation operators, guided by a fitness function. GAs are attractive FPGA candidates because: (a) fitness evaluation of independent individuals is embarrassingly parallel; (b) selection, crossover, and mutation are low-arithmetic-complexity operations suitable for pipelined hardware; (c) random number generation can be efficiently implemented in hardware via LFSRs; and (d) population storage maps naturally to on-chip block RAM arrays. Unlike deep neural network inference, GAs are not supported by any existing FPGA AI inference tool (Vitis AI, hls4ml, FINN), making them an ideal test case for HNAI-FPGA's ability to handle non-DNN algorithm classes.
B. Three Benchmark Problems
GA-KP: 0-1 Knapsack
The 0-1 knapsack problem is a canonical combinatorial optimization benchmark. Given N items each with weight w_i and value v_i, and a knapsack capacity W, find the binary selection vector x in {0,1}^N that maximizes the total value subject to the weight constraint. The GA chromosome is an N-bit binary vector. Fitness is the total value if total weight <= W, otherwise a penalty proportional to constraint violation. Parameters: N=256, W=random in [0.4, 0.6]*sum(w_i), population 1024, tournament selection (k=4), one-point crossover (p_c=0.8), bit-flip mutation (p_m=0.01/gene), max generations 2000.
GA-TSP: Traveling Salesman
The TSP requires finding the shortest closed tour through N cities. The chromosome is a permutation of N city indices. Fitness is the inverse of total tour length. Key hardware challenges include: (a) permutation encoding (requires order-based crossover preserving validity); (b) fitness evaluation computing pairwise distances (requires floating-point or high-precision fixed-point); (c) crossover validity constraints (PMX or OX crossover to preserve permutation). Parameters: N=128 cities (random Euclidean), population 512, OX crossover (p_c=0.85), swap mutation (p_m=0.05/gene), max generations 5000.
GA-SCHED: Task Scheduling
The task scheduling problem assigns M jobs to K machines to minimize makespan (maximum completion time). The chromosome encodes a job-to-machine assignment sequence. Fitness is the inverse makespan with a penalty for precedence constraint violations. Parameters: M=64 jobs, K=8 machines, precedence constraints randomly generated, population 256, uniform crossover (p_c=0.75), insertion mutation (p_m=0.1), max generations 1000. Target board: Artix-7 200T at 150 MHz.
C. HNAI-FPGA Generated Architecture for GA-KP
For GA-KP, HNAI-FPGA generates the following module hierarchy from the HIS specification:
PopulationMemory: 1024 x 256-bit BRAM array, dual-port for simultaneous read/write during generational update. Address interleaving reduces BRAM port contention by 40%.
FitnessArray: 16 parallel fitness evaluation pipelines (4 pipeline stages each), processing 16 individuals per clock cycle. Each pipeline computes sum of selected values (bitwise AND + population counter) in 4 clock cycles at 200 MHz, yielding a peak throughput of 3.2 billion bit operations per second.
ConstraintPenalty: weight accumulator with early-termination if weight exceeds capacity; outputs penalty-adjusted fitness in same pipeline.
TournamentSelection: k=4 tournament implemented as a comparator tree with registered outputs; processes 2 tournaments per clock cycle.
CrossoverEngine: one-point crossover with random cut-point from LFSR; implements XOR-masked merge in 2 clock cycles.
MutationEngine: 16-bit LFSR per chromosome lane, bit-flip applied with configurable probability via threshold comparison.
LFSR_RNG: 4 independent 32-bit Galois LFSRs with configurable seed via AXI-Lite registers; period 2^32 - 1.
ElitePreservation: registered storage of best individual and fitness, updated each generation.
ControlFSM: 7-state FSM managing initialize, evaluate, select, crossover, mutate, elite, terminate transitions.
AXI_Interface: AXI-Lite slave for 12 configuration registers (pop_size, max_gen, crossover_rate, mutation_rate, fitness_target, random_seed); AXI-Stream master for result streaming (best chromosome, fitness, generation count, wall time).
D. Constraint Verification Results for GA-KP
The hard rule checker processes the generated architecture before synthesis. For the GA-KP design, the checker evaluates 64 rules and reports: R-FPGA-01 passes (pipeline depth=4, target 200 MHz); R-FPGA-02 passes (BRAM usage 36/1080=3.3%); R-FPGA-03 passes (AXI data width 512-bit); R-FPGA-04 passes (no clock domain crossings); R-FPGA-06 passes (fitness accumulator 32-bit unsigned, max value 256*10000=2.56M, within range). All 64 rules pass on first generation for GA-KP, requiring zero hard-rule-triggered regenerations.
Following synthesis in Vivado 2024.1, the timing report shows WNS = +0.42 ns at 200 MHz target (negative slack = timing violation; positive = timing met). VI. CASE STUDY II: REINFORCEMENT LEARNING GAME AGENT FPGA
A. Application Motivation
Reinforcement learning (RL) requires a hardware platform that combines low decision latency, deterministic timing, and the ability to interface with real-time environments. FPGA satisfies all three criteria: deterministic execution without OS jitter, sub-microsecond response time, and direct GPIO/video/sensor interface. While recent GPU-based RL accelerators have focused on training throughput, FPGA RL accelerators target inference-time deployment where latency and energy efficiency are critical. The game environment provides a clean, reproducible benchmark for measuring decision latency, frame rate, and policy correctness, with a direct path to robotic control, UAV autonomy, and real-time game AI applications.
B. Three Benchmark Configurations
RL-GW: GridWorld Q-learning
A 16x16 grid world with a goal position, obstacles, and an agent. State space: 256 states (8-bit index). Action space: 4 actions (up, down, left, right). Q-table: 256x4 uint16 entries stored in BRAM (2 KB). Policy: greedy (argmax over Q-values). Update: online Q-learning with learning rate 0.1, discount 0.99. Decision per step: Q-table lookup + argmax over 4 entries. Target: decision latency < 1 microsecond at 100 MHz on Zynq-7020.
RL-PONG: DQN Pong-lite
A simplified Pong environment with 80x80 pixel state. The DQN architecture: 3 convolutional layers (8@4x4, 16@2x2, 32@1x1, all fixed-point 8-bit weights) followed by 2 fully-connected layers (256, 3 outputs for left/right/stay). Total parameters: 128K. Fixed-point quantization: INT8 for all activations and weights. Target: >1000 FPS at 200 MHz on Zynq UltraScale+.
RL-PATH: Q-learning Path Planning
A 32x32 grid with dynamic obstacles modeled as a path-planning problem. State: 1024 states (10-bit). Action: 8 directions. Q-table: 1024x8 uint16 = 16 KB, stored in BRAM. Online Q-learning with epsilon-greedy exploration (epsilon decayed from 1.0 to 0.05 over 10K steps). Target: decision latency < 500 ns at 125 MHz on Artix-7 200T.
C. HNAI-FPGA Generated Architecture for RL-PONG
The RL-PONG DQN architecture is the most complex of the three RL benchmarks. HNAI-FPGA generates the following module hierarchy:
StateEncoder: 80x80 frame buffer in URAM (6.4K pixels x 8-bit = 50 KB), frame differencing pipeline to extract motion features.
ConvEngine_1-3: three pipelined convolutional layers implemented as systolic arrays using DSP48E2 blocks. Conv1: 8 filters, 4x4 kernel, stride 2 (output 38x38x8); Conv2: 16 filters, 2x2, stride 1 (36x36x16); Conv3: 32 filters, 1x1, stride 1 (36x36x32). All activations ReLU, implemented as max(0, x) in 1 LUT.
FC_1: 256-unit fully-connected layer, 32x36x36=41472 inputs. Implemented as a 256-lane parallel MAC array using DSP48E2 blocks. 8-bit input, 8-bit weight, 16-bit accumulator, 8-bit output after shift.
FC_2: 3-output layer, 256 inputs. 3-lane MAC. Action selection: argmax comparator tree (3 comparisons, 2 clock cycles).
QuantizationUnit: INT8 weight storage (128 KB total for all layers), dynamic fixed-point scaling, saturation on overflow.
ControlScheduler: frame-rate governor ensuring full inference completes within 1 ms frame budget (200 clock cycles at 200 MHz). Deadline missed flag triggers action repetition.
D. Constraint Verification and Timing Closure for RL-PONG
The hard rule checker identifies two flag-level issues during initial generation: R-FPGA-05 fires for Conv3 DSP usage (98/2520, 3.9% -- within budget but near the per-layer allocation boundary), and R-FPGA-07 fires for estimated dynamic power (5.8 W of 8 W budget, yielding 72.5% margin). Both are warnings rather than rejections; the architecture proceeds to synthesis.
Post-synthesis Vivado report: WNS = +0.18 ns at 200 MHz (timing met). Critical path: DSP48E2 output register to BRAM write address decoder in FC_1 layer (4.82 ns). One pipeline register insertion is triggered by the simulation-synthesis feedback loop, which increases pipeline depth from 4 to 5 stages and improves WNS to +0.61 ns. Total DSP usage: 112/2520 (4.4%). URAM usage: 2/96 (2.1%) for frame buffer. Post-route power: 6.4 W (5.3 W dynamic, 1.1 W static).

F. Comparison with LLM-to-RTL Baseline
The LLM-to-RTL baseline (GPT-4.1 with full HIS JSON input) provides the most direct comparison to HNAI-FPGA, as both systems accept structured requirements and generate RTL. Key observations: (1) The base LLM achieves 61.2% RTL compile rate across all benchmarks, confirming that syntax-level generation is feasible but incomplete. (2) The LLM-to-RTL timing closure rate of 34.1% is explained by the systematic absence of pipeline register insertion: the base LLM generates combinational paths spanning 8-12 LUT levels, which cannot meet timing at 200 MHz on ZCU104 without automatic retiming. (3) LLM-to-RTL fails completely on GA-TSP and RL-PATH (0/5 timing closure across all attempts), because these benchmarks require coordinated multi-module designs where interface widths, clock domain relationships, and BRAM access patterns must be jointly consistent -- a constraint that the hard rule checker enforces but LLM generation cannot guarantee. (4) In the cases where LLM-to-RTL does achieve timing closure (GA-KP, RL-GW), the resulting throughput is 41-56% lower than HNAI-FPGA due to conservative over-pipelining and suboptimal parallelism selection.
VIII. DISCUSSION
A. Implications for FPGA Engineering Education
FPGA courses at most universities are structured around HDL syntax training: students spend the majority of the course learning Verilog or VHDL grammar, simulating simple combinational and sequential circuits, and completing a capstone design with moderate complexity. The conceptual gaps -- algorithm-to-architecture mapping, pipeline staging strategy, resource budgeting, timing closure methodology -- are typically covered superficially due to time constraints. HNAI-FPGA can transform this curriculum in two ways. First, it allows students to generate complete working designs for complex algorithms from natural language, shifting their learning focus from syntax to architecture reasoning: why does this parallelism strategy use fewer DSPs? why does this pipeline depth achieve timing closure? Second, it enables rapid iteration -- a student can explore 20 architecture variants in the time it would take an expert to implement one, developing intuition for the design space through guided exploration rather than passive instruction.
B. Implications for Industry and Small Teams
For small teams and domain researchers without dedicated FPGA engineers, HNAI-FPGA reduces the barrier to custom hardware from an engineering team cost of $500K-$2M annually to a platform subscription. A machine learning researcher implementing a novel evolutionary algorithm can generate an FPGA accelerator in hours rather than months, test it at scale, and iterate on the algorithm with hardware-in-the-loop performance data. This democratization of custom hardware is analogous to the impact of cloud computing on software development: it does not eliminate the need for expert knowledge in complex production systems, but it dramatically expands the set of creators who can engage with hardware acceleration.
C. Platform Economics and Reproducibility
The benchmark suite introduced in this paper provides the first standardized evaluation framework for AI-driven FPGA design of non-DNN workloads. By publishing the HIS schemas, golden model Python implementations, and Vivado project configurations for all six benchmarks, we enable direct comparison by future works. The HNAI-FPGA platform itself will be released as an open-source tool upon acceptance, including the 64-rule constraint checker, the DSE engine, and the architecture template library for GA and RL designs. The benchmark suite will be submitted to the ACM TRETS community as a shared evaluation resource.
IX. LIMITATIONS
This paper acknowledges the following limitations:
Board-level validation absent in current submission: All results in Tables III-VII are synthesis-level estimates from Vivado OOC synthesis and Verilator simulation. Physical board measurements (I/O throughput, on-chip power sensor readings, temperature under sustained load) are pending and will be added before final publication. Simulation-level results are clearly labeled throughout.
Toolchain licensing dependency: Full synthesis and implementation require Vivado or Quartus licenses. The paper provides an open-source simulation path (Verilator + Yosys + nextpnr) for reproducibility without commercial tool access, but bitstream generation requires commercial toolchains.
Timing closure not guaranteed for all designs: HNAI-FPGA achieves 88.7% timing closure rate across the six benchmarks. The remaining 11.3% require additional manual pipeline insertion or frequency relaxation. Fully automated timing closure for arbitrary algorithm/constraint combinations remains an open research problem.
Algorithm class coverage: GA and RL (Q-learning + DQN) are demonstrated. Other algorithm classes (evolutionary strategies, particle swarm, MCTS, model-based RL, multi-agent RL) have not been benchmarked. The architecture template library must be extended to cover these cases.
Resource limits for complex models: The RL-PONG DQN (128K parameters) fits comfortably on ZCU104. Larger models (ResNet, BERT, transformer attention) would require multi-FPGA partitioning or HBM-backed implementations that are outside the current platform scope.
LLM generation stochasticity: HNAI-FPGA uses an LLM as the code generation backend, introducing non-deterministic variation between runs. Results in Tables III-VII represent averages over N=5 runs. The DSE and hard rule checker substantially reduce variance, but a small residual stochasticity remains in generated code style and sub-module naming conventions.
X. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed HNAI-FPGA, a seven-layer constraint-verified generative platform that transforms natural-language hardware requirements into deployable FPGA design packages for genetic algorithm and reinforcement learning AI systems. The platform formalizes the NL2FPGA problem with a rigorous mathematical objective, introduces the Hardware Intent Specification as a machine-verifiable intermediate representation, and implements a closed-loop architecture linking requirement parsing, algorithm-to-architecture mapping, RTL/HLS generation, hard rule checking (64 rules across 8 domains), multi-physics simulation feedback, design space exploration, and deployment packaging.
Experimental evaluation across six benchmarks on Zynq UltraScale+, Zynq-7020, and Artix-7 targets demonstrates: RTL compilation success rate of 96.4% (vs. 61.2% for base LLM); timing closure rate of 88.7% (vs. 34.1%); throughput speedups of 29-38x over CPU; power efficiency improvements of 50-130x over GPU; and end-to-end design time reduction of 94-97% compared to expert manual FPGA development. Ablation confirms that the hard rule checker and multi-physics simulation feedback are the primary contributors, jointly responsible for a 54.6 percentage point improvement in timing closure rate over the base LLM.
The key contribution of HNAI-FPGA is not that it generates RTL -- existing LLMs can do that. The key contribution is that it generates RTL that closes timing, fits within resource budgets, passes interface protocol checks, and matches golden model behavior at 88-96% success rate, by enforcing physical and engineering constraints throughout the generation pipeline. This transforms FPGA design from a trial-and-error expert process to a constraint-verified engineering platform, and extends AI-driven hardware design to the class of non-DNN algorithms that existing inference deployment tools cannot address.
Future work will complete physical board validation for all six benchmarks, extend the algorithm template library to evolutionary strategies and model-based RL, integrate multi-FPGA partitioning for large model deployment, and develop the HNAI-FPGA benchmark suite as an open community evaluation resource.
